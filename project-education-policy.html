§ 01
The Problem

Education policy treats emotion as a side note.

"Educational policy often treats emotion as a secondary concern, separate from academic learning. This project argues that emotion is not an 'extra' in education — it is a core part of the learning system."

  1. 1.01
    Education policy focuses heavily on academic outcomes, but often ignores emotional conditions in classrooms.
  2. 1.02
    Students' stress, disengagement, and behavioral issues are increasing — and traditional fixes are not working.
  3. 1.03
    Teachers are expected to manage emotional labor without structured support or training.
  4. 1.04
    Existing federal policy (ESSA) has the structural space, but no clear priorities for emotional learning.

§ 04
Key Findings

What the research actually shows.

Three findings that anchor the policy case — drawn from psychology, neuroscience, and labor economics.

01

Emotion shapes learning outcomes.

— Finding 04.01 · Developmental Psychology

When teachers are warm, fair, and responsive, students stay engaged, try harder, and persist through difficulty. Emotional support is not a "nice extra" — it is part of how learning works.
02

Stress interferes with how the brain learns.

— Finding 04.02 · Cognitive Neuroscience

Stress affects attention, working memory, and self-control — the exact skills needed to listen, solve, and complete tasks. When emotional conditions are poor, learning becomes harder at a biological level.
03

Teacher well-being is a system variable.

— Finding 04.03 · Labor & Workforce Research

Teachers manage instruction, behavior, conflict, and emotional needs — under high stress and financial pressure. When teachers are exhausted, the entire classroom climate suffers.
§ 05
Recommendations

Four articles, all within ESSA.

No new legislation. Each recommendation works inside the existing framework.

I.

Prioritize relationship-building skills in Title II teacher training.

Title II already funds professional development, but doesn't explicitly emphasize relational and emotional support skills. Make these competencies — running structured discussions, building communication routines, responding to conflict constructively — a clear training priority.

— The goal isn't adding work, but using existing training time more effectively.
II.

Shift from one-time workshops to sustained practice.

Teachers need ongoing opportunities to practice emotional skills, discuss real classroom challenges, and receive feedback — not just isolated workshops. This can be integrated into existing school meetings without creating new structures.

— Teaching emotional skills requires practice, not just information.
III.

Use Title IV-A for systemic climate improvement.

Title IV-A supports "safe and supportive environments," but is often used for isolated programs. Build school-wide systems that reduce conflict, implement consistent discipline approaches, and focus on relationship repair rather than punishment.

— This creates more stable, supportive learning environments.
IV.

Treat teacher compensation and well-being as education infrastructure.

Teacher well-being is often framed as personal, but it's fundamentally tied to policy decisions about pay, benefits, and workload. When teachers are financially stable and supported, they can sustain the patience and presence that teaching requires.

— Teacher well-being isn't a luxury — it's foundational infrastructure for learning.
§ 06
Reflection

What this taught me about policy.

— Personal annotation Writing a Senate testimony was the moment I realized policy isn't about finding the most clever argument. It's about making feasible recommendations stick.

Writing the issue statement pushed me to synthesize evidence across economics, developmental science, and public policy. I had to move beyond cataloguing studies and build a coherent argument: that ending programs like the Affordable Connectivity Program wasn't saving money — it was deferring larger costs onto vulnerable students and communities.

The testimony format was also new to me. Translating academic research into a persuasive oral statement for a Senate committee required anticipating counterarguments, foregrounding human stakes, and making recommendations realistic within existing frameworks. I chose to argue from within ESSA rather than calling for new legislation, because effective policy often works with existing structures rather than against institutional inertia.

Writing the personal section reminded me that policy analysis isn't purely technical — it's shaped by lived experience. This project strengthened my ability to synthesize cross-disciplinary research and communicate findings to non-academic audiences, and deepened my interest in the intersection of human development and institutional design.
