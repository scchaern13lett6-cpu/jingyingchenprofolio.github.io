Project 02 · Survey-Based Research

Pre-Sleep Cognitive Arousal, Imagination, and Anxiety.

Not all pre-sleep thinking is harmless — this study examines how fantasy imagination before bed shapes anxiety, sleep quality, and emotional responses to uncertainty.

Author Jingying Chen
Methodology Survey · ANOVA · Regression
Sample Size N = 134
Tools SPSS · JASP · Prolific
★ Keywords pre-sleep cognition GAD-7 anxiety imagination sleep latency phone use
— Abstract

A quantitative survey study examining how pre-sleep imagination relates to anxiety and sleep health. Using GAD-7 anxiety scores and one-way ANOVA across 134 participants, results suggest that not all pre-sleep cognition is harmless — fantasy frequency operates on a threshold, and phone use compounds with imagination to delay sleep onset more than either alone.

§ 01
Background

Do you fantasize before falling asleep?

— The research question

This study investigated whether pre-sleep imagination — from daydreaming to "what if" scenarios — is actually linked to anxiety, and how age and bedtime habits shape the experience. The goal was to move past intuition. Most of us have heard that screens disrupt sleep, but what about thinking itself? What kinds of pre-sleep mental activity are protective, and which ones are quietly harmful?

Using survey data from 134 participants recruited via Prolific and social media, this study applied the GAD-7 anxiety scale and one-way ANOVA analyses across three research questions. The dataset captured self-reported imagination frequency, type, and duration; sleep latency and quality; phone use and reading habits before bed; and emotional response to a sudden weather change — used as a behavioral proxy for psychological flexibility.

§ 03
Key Findings

Three things I learned.

Not all pre-sleep thinking is created equal — frequency, behavior, and timing each shape outcomes differently.

01

Fantasy & anxiety: it's about threshold, not degree.

— Finding 01 · Frequency Effect

Only participants who fantasized every night showed significantly elevated anxiety scores. The mean for nightly fantasizers reached M = 10.93, compared to M = 4.71 for those who rarely did. The three lower-frequency groups did not significantly differ from each other.

This suggests anxiety is not a linear function of imagination — it kicks in at a threshold of habitual engagement.

F(3, 108) = 5.78 · p = .001 · η² = .138
02

Phone use + imagination: compounding effects on sleep onset.

— Finding 02 · Interaction Effect

Phone use before bed alone delayed sleep onset. Imagination alone also delayed it. But participants who did both experienced a delay greater than either factor individually — the combination was worse than the sum of its parts.

This points to a behavioral interaction: screens prime a particular kind of mental activity, and that pairing extends sleep latency further than either intervention would predict on its own.

Behavior × Behavior interaction
03

Poor sleep predicts longer imagination.

— Finding 03 · Bidirectional Loop

Participants reporting poor sleep quality engaged in longer pre-sleep imagination compared to those reporting good sleep quality. This direction matters: imagination may not just disrupt sleep — poor sleep may also drive more imagination the next night.

The pattern suggests a potential bidirectional loop, where each side reinforces the other across consecutive nights.

Bidirectional relationship · Multi-night pattern
§ 04
Implications

Pre-sleep cognition is not neutral.

The findings suggest that the type and frequency of mental activity before bed shapes both anxiety levels and emotional resilience. "Thinking of nothing" before sleep was associated with faster sleep onset and greater adaptability, while fantasy immersion and phone use showed the opposite pattern.

This research adds to the growing body of evidence that mental health interventions should consider not just what people worry about, but how and when they engage their imagination.

§ 05
Reflection

What I learned doing this.

— Lab notebook entry · Post-submission

This study gave me hands-on experience designing and executing a quantitative survey from scratch — writing items, recruiting participants through Prolific, and running ANOVAs in JASP. The process taught me how much decisions made early in survey design constrain what you can later analyze.

Several limitations stood out in hindsight: the sample was relatively small and skewed toward younger adults, and I didn't control for existing mental health diagnoses, which likely influenced anxiety scores. Some questions were also too broad — "phone use before bed" could mean very different things from one participant to another.

Going forward, I'm interested in following up with longitudinal designs or experience sampling methods to capture pre-sleep cognition in real time, rather than relying on retrospective self-report. I'd also want to distinguish between adaptive and maladaptive fantasy — to better understand when imagination helps versus hurts.

More broadly, this project taught me that quantitative research is as much about asking the right question as it is about running the right test. The cleanest analysis can't fix a poorly framed survey item.
