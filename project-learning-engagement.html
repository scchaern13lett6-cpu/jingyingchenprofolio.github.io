Improving ADHD Writing Experience.

— The Brief

"Design a solution that improves ADHD students' writing experience — without relying on traditional instructional approaches."

— Lee, week 1
§ 01 · V1 (failed)
First Iteration

What didn't work.

— Going by the textbook. The "right" answer turned out to be the wrong one.

I followed conventional educational strategies — and watched each one fall apart in client review.

▸ STRATEGY 01
Task breakdown

Smaller steps still required initiation, which is the actual bottleneck.

▸ STRATEGY 02
Multiple deadlines

Self-imposed deadlines had no real enforcement.

▸ STRATEGY 03
Free writing

Rarely translated into final drafts.

▸ STRATEGY 04
Group reflection

Required emotional energy ADHD learners often don't have.

— Realization

Strategies that are pedagogically "correct" are not necessarily behaviorally effective for ADHD learners.

§ 03 · V2 (working)
Three Iterations

What works.

Not magic — just systems that compensate for executive dysfunction. Note: still might not work for every ADHD profile.

Iteration A 01 ★ TESTED

Dopamine-Driven System.

I designed a system that integrates motivation directly into the writing process — not as a reward at the end.

  • 7 : 3 structure (70% work, 30% reward)
  • Rewards must be highly engaging and pre-selected
  • Emphasis on immediate reinforcement, not delayed gratification
Replace willpower with structured incentive.
Iteration B 02 ★ TESTED

"Locked Writing" Environment.

A writing interface designed specifically for ADHD — environment doing the work willpower can't.

  • Locks the screen to prevent distraction
  • Real-time word count, time spent, progress
  • Self-competition (historical best output)
  • Symbolic rewards (visual badges)
Replace self-control with environmental constraint & feedback.
Iteration C 03 ★ TESTED

Externalized Accountability.

If internal executive function is unreliable, build the executive function outside the person.

  • Shared scheduling tools for co-working
  • Enables "parallel work" rather than collaboration
  • Body-doubling reduces task initiation friction
  • Visibility creates social accountability
Compensate for weak internal executive function.
§ 04
So...

What this project actually taught me.

ADHD challenges are not primarily about ability. They are about execution and motivation systems.
Effective design must account for real behavior, not ideal behavior.
Empathy is not just understanding users — it requires challenging your own assumptions as a designer.
Learning design can borrow from product and behavioral design thinking — and probably should.
§ 05
Reflection

Failure was the design.

— Notebook entry, post-V1 I keep wanting to call this a "lesson learned" but it really was just watching every assumption I had about teaching get politely demolished.

This project fundamentally reshaped how I think about learning design. I initially approached ADHD writing challenges with conventional strategies like task breakdown and deadline setting, but quickly realized these methods failed to translate into actual behavior.

Through iteration and feedback, I learned that effective design must align with how users actually function, not how they are expected to function. Shifting toward a dopamine-driven, motivation-based framework allowed me to better address task initiation and sustained focus. I also recognized the importance of questioning my own assumptions and embracing failure as part of the design process.

Ultimately, this project taught me to move beyond instruction and think in terms of behavioral systems, where environment, feedback, and motivation play a central role in shaping user outcomes — and where the most "obvious" pedagogical answer is often the one that quietly fails when it leaves the textbook.
